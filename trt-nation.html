Frequently Asked Questions

Who is a candidate for TRT?
Men with consistent symptoms of low testosterone—such as low energy, reduced libido, fatigue, muscle loss, or mood changes—and confirmed low testosterone levels on lab work may be candidates. TRT Nation requires lab testing and a physician review before prescribing. It is not suitable for men with certain prostate or breast conditions, untreated sleep apnea, or elevated red blood cell counts.
What lab tests are required?
A comprehensive hormone panel typically includes total testosterone, free testosterone, estradiol, SHBG, PSA, CBC, and metabolic markers. These labs establish your baseline and help your physician rule out contraindications. Follow-up labs are scheduled periodically to monitor your response and adjust your protocol.
How long before I notice results?
Many men report improved energy, libido, and mood within 2-4 weeks of starting therapy. Changes in muscle mass, strength, and body composition typically become more noticeable after 8-12 weeks. Individual results depend on your starting levels, dosage, lifestyle, and adherence to the protocol.
Are there side effects?
Possible side effects include acne, fluid retention, increased red blood cell count, changes in mood, reduced fertility, and gynecomastia (breast tissue growth). Regular labs and physician monitoring help catch and manage these issues early. Always report new symptoms to your provider.
Can TRT affect fertility?
Yes, exogenous testosterone can suppress sperm production and reduce fertility in some men. If preserving fertility is important to you, discuss HCG or alternative protocols with your TRT Nation physician before starting treatment. They can tailor a plan that aligns with your family-planning goals.
Is testosterone a steroid?
Testosterone is technically an anabolic-androgenic steroid because it is a naturally occurring hormone with both anabolic (tissue-building) and androgenic (male-characteristic) effects. However, medically supervised TRT is not the same as anabolic steroid abuse. TRT aims to restore testosterone to healthy physiological levels, not supraphysiological doses used by some athletes.
